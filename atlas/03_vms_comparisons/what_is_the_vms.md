### [HISTORICAL]

Before examining interpretations, theories, evidence, or conclusions, we must first understand the object itself.

The manuscript at the center of this Atlas is known today as the Voynich Manuscript, commonly abbreviated as VMS.

It is one of the most famous and controversial manuscripts in the world.

For more than a century, researchers have debated its language, purpose, authorship, and meaning.

Countless theories have been proposed.

Few have achieved lasting acceptance.

The result is a manuscript surrounded by both scholarship and mythology.

The purpose of this chapter is to separate the two.

### The Physical Manuscript

The Voynich Manuscript is currently housed at the Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library at Yale University.

It is cataloged as:

MS 408

The manuscript consists primarily of vellum pages containing:

- Botanical illustrations
- Astronomical diagrams
- Human figures
- Pharmaceutical imagery
- Dense handwritten text
- Complex foldouts

The text is written in an unknown script that has resisted conventional decipherment efforts for generations.

### The Radiocarbon Date

One of the most important facts concerning the manuscript is its radiocarbon dating.

Scientific testing of the vellum conducted by researchers led by Greg Hodgins and published by Zyats and colleagues placed the parchment within the early fifteenth century.

The resulting range is commonly reported as approximately:

1404–1438 CE

This date is critical because it establishes the manuscript as a genuine medieval artifact rather than a modern forgery.

It also places the manuscript within the same historical period discussed throughout the earlier chapters of this Atlas.

### Why It Is Called the Voynich Manuscript

The manuscript is named after Wilfrid Voynich, a rare book dealer who acquired it in 1912.

Voynich did not create the manuscript.

He did not translate it.

He did not identify its author.

His name became attached to the manuscript because he introduced it to the modern scholarly world.

The title therefore functions as a historical identifier rather than a statement of authorship.

Throughout this Atlas, the manuscript will be referred to as:

Voynich Manuscript (VMS)

or simply:

VMS

### The Structure of the Manuscript

Researchers traditionally divide the manuscript into several major sections based upon illustration style and content.

Although interpretations differ, the visual divisions themselves are widely recognized.

For purposes of this Atlas, the manuscript is organized as follows:

#### §H — Herbal Section

Plant illustrations accompanied by extensive text.

#### §A — Astronomical Section

Circular diagrams, wheels, zodiac imagery, and celestial layouts.

#### §B — Bath Section

Human figures interacting with pools, vessels, and fluid systems.

#### §C — Nine-Rosette Foldout

Large multi-page diagram containing interconnected circular structures.

#### §P — Pharmaceutical Section

Containers, roots, preparation imagery, and associated text.

#### §T — Prescription Codex

Dense textual pages featuring repeated structural patterns and marker symbols.

These labels serve as navigational tools throughout the remainder of the book.

### Folio References

Like many medieval manuscripts, the VMS is referenced by folio rather than page number.

Each sheet has two sides:

- r = recto (front)
- v = verso (back)

Thus:

- f1r = Folio 1, front side
- f1v = Folio 1, reverse side
- f57v = Folio 57, reverse side
- f116v = Folio 116, reverse side

These references allow precise identification of manuscript locations.

Throughout Atlas, all manuscript citations use this convention.

### The Unknown Script

The most famous feature of the manuscript is its script.

Thousands of words appear throughout the text.

The characters are written fluently and consistently.

The writing exhibits many statistical properties associated with natural language.

Yet no universally accepted translation exists.

Over the years researchers have proposed:

- Enciphered language
- Constructed language
- Shorthand system
- Hoax
- Glossolalia
- Mnemonic notation
- Multiple-language mixtures

The debate remains ongoing.

### The Problem of Interpretation

The VMS presents an unusual challenge.

The manuscript contains both text and imagery.

Interpretations must therefore account for both.

A successful explanation should ideally explain:

- The script
- The illustrations
- The section structure
- The statistical properties
- The manuscript's historical context

Any theory that explains only one of these elements remains incomplete.

### The Challenge of Mythology

Over time, the manuscript accumulated a substantial folklore tradition.

Claims have connected it to:

- Secret societies
- Lost civilizations
- Extraterrestrials
- Alchemy
- Hidden treasures
- Mystical codes

Most such claims lack supporting evidence.

The popularity of the manuscript often encourages speculation.

This Atlas adopts a different approach.

The emphasis will remain on evidence, documentation, comparison, and reproducibility.

Every serious investigation begins by defining its subject.

Before asking what the manuscript means, we must understand what the manuscript is.

Before evaluating claims, we must establish terminology.

Before considering interpretations, we must establish evidence.
